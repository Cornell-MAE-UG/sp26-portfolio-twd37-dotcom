## Context and Problem Statement

Over the past decade, spotted lanternflies have become a major issue for vineyards across the United States, affecting both grape quality and harvest quantity. Our team focused on the problem of SLF mixing with grapes during harvesting and explored density separation in sugar water or grape juice as a potential solution. This direction was selected because it could improve the harvested grape ratio, work quickly in the existing process, and rely on accessible materials.

## Final Prototype and Application

Our final prototype is a grape-juice or sugar-water-based separation system that uses density differences to separate SLF from grapes immediately after harvest. After grapes and SLF are shaken loose, the mixture moves into the separator. Grapes sink and fall through an opening in the primary container, while SLF float and are scooped into a separate container using a water-wheel-like assembly. The liquid is then recirculated using pumps.

At full implementation scale, we envision the device sitting on top of the gondola traveling beside the harvester so separation happens in the field without major equipment changes.

## Conclusion and Recommendation

Our team believes the design is promising and worth further development, but it still needs more advanced testing before field use. Sugar-water concentration could be tuned to match grape sugar content more closely, and future iterations should also account for cleaning, pump reliability, and compatibility with different harvester designs.

Client feedback supported the concept and suggested practical improvements, including using free-run juice instead of water, handling leaves and debris during the process, and making the device removable when not needed.

## Testing and Results

One of our main testing goals during prototyping was evaluating motion, interference, and connection strength between the fabricated parts. After assembling and mounting the wheel into the prototype container, we found that the wheel could not complete a full rotation because it rubbed against the tapered bucket walls.

We also tested the stability of the mounted assembly and found that the wheel slipped and came apart too easily. Based on those results, we concluded that future versions should use epoxy for permanent connections and a rectangular untapered container to avoid unnecessary friction and wear.

## Prototype and Testing Details

The prototype was constructed using two 3D-printed plastic wheels, a 1-inch outer diameter hex bar shaft, and two aluminum fins cut from perforated sheet stock. The wheel assembly was mounted into a modified container with cutouts for shaft support and an opening for water and grapes to pass through.
