Team Name: LanternBye

Team Members: Katie Kim, Julia Cho, Daasabre McPhee-Djan, Toby Young, Tim Duffy

Context & Problem Statement: 

Over the past decade, spotted lantern flies (SLF) have become a large issue for vineyards across the United States, impacting both grape quality and harvest quantity. In order to tackle this problem, our team chose to specifically focus on the issue of SLF mixing with grapes during the harvesting process as the shaker dislodges both ripe grapes and SLF from vines using the concept of density separation in sugar water or grape juice. This sub-problem was chosen due to the fact that a solution to this issue would ensure a higher quality ratio of grape harvest, be a quick, effective process, and would only require easily accessible resources for actual fabrication (e.g. sugar water or grape juice). Our decision was shaped considering several constraints, including available materials, fabrication time, costs, the loss of grapes’ sugar content in water, and the short time frame (between vine to collection bins) this density separation would have to occur in. 

Final Prototype & Application:

Our final prototype is a grape juice or sugar water-based separation system that utilizes density differences to separate SLF from grapes immediately after harvest. After the mechanical shaker of a harvester dislodges both grapes and SLF from vines, this mixture will be taken via conveyor belt to the collection gondola, on top of which our density separator will be fastened. Grapes will sink and fall through an opening in the bottom of the primary container, which will be slanted at ~10º, as SLF float to the top of the solution and are scooped into a separate container using a ‘water-wheel’-like design. After the grapes and liquid fall through the opening, they will enter a second container with holes drilled into the bottom in order to strain the grapes from the solution. Finally, the solution will be pumped back into the initial container in order to provide a controlled stream.

At an actual implementation level in vineyards, we envision our design to be placed directly on top of the gondola traveling next to the harvester machines to ensure all harvested grapes and SLF go through this separation process. One benefit of our design is that since all the water or juice being used in this process will be recirculated using a pump, harvesters will only need to be prepped with one ‘batch’ of liquid before going into the field to harvest. To finish the harvesting process, grapes that are fully separated from the sugar water and SLF will be dumped into collection bins to be sent for processing.

Conclusion & Recommendation:

Our team believes that this design is promising and worth further development, but requires more advanced testing to be made field ready. Firstly, sugar water was implemented to prevent the possibility of diluting the sugar content in the grapes. While this isn’t a large concern due to the short period of time the grapes spend submerged in water, the ratio of sugar to water in the solution can be fine-tuned to match the sugar content of individual types of grapes to eliminate this risk entirely. We believe that potentially using the grape slush mixture mentioned by the wineries could also work well as we would not have to change or alter sugar concentrations as they should be extremely close. Some foreseeable problems with this is the potential for our pumps to get jammed or sticky after use. By using specific pumps that have good filtration systems or are easy to clean this could be fixed. Furthermore, more in-depth design work will have to be done in order to ensure that our design can be implemented into the type of harvester each vineyard uses. However, with our initial results and design tweaks based on the prototyping phase, our design is structurally sound, water retentive, and runs smoothly for efficient energy consumption, which suggests that this design is promising for field implementation.

Jennifer Russo confirmed that the idea is new and not something she or her colleagues had considered before. She suggested a few practical improvements, like using free-run juice instead of water to protect grape quality, allowing leaves and debris (MOG) to be removed during the process, and placing the device on top of the gondola so sorting can happen in the field without changing the harvester. She also noted that the device should be easy to remove when it’s not needed. Overall, her feedback supports the idea and gives clear ways to improve it.

Testing & Results:

One of the main focuses of our team during the prototyping phase was testing the motion, interference, and connections of our machined and ordered components. This was tested in order to ensure the smooth operation of our assembly parts as a larger design, such as to make sure that there is no clashing, grinding, etc. between components. This was tested by assembling all components, mounting them to our separation container, and spinning the entire assembly with a handle to see if the design functions properly within the full range expected (360º). After assembling all of the components and installing these pieces into the clear container we were using in our prototype, we observed that the wheel could not fully rotate because it grinded against the walls of the plastic bucket at the bottom due to the bucket’s taper. Therefore, we determined that for our final design, a rectangular, untapered container will have to be purchased for this initial separation container to prevent wear and unnecessary energy consumption and allow for ease of movement.

To ensure that the design and assembly of our ‘water wheel’ is stable and well-supported,we tested our prototype by assembling the two acrylic wheels, PVC pipe, and two aluminum fins as described in the procedures in the following section. Then, this complete assembly was mounted onto our initial container for separation in two cutout slots and was checked for sliding, shifting, or catching as it was rotated within the full range. After installing this assembly into the clear container we are using for our prototype, we observed that the assembly kept slipping and fell apart easily. Therefore, our team determined that the components must be sealed together using epoxy, which will also make the design stronger and more resistant to environmental wear during use.

Prototype & Testing Details:

Our prototype was constructed using two 3D printed ⅛” thick plastic wheels, a 1” outer diameter hex bar shaft, and two aluminum ‘fins’ cut from an aluminum perforated sheet. Firstly, two plastic wheels were 3D printed to include a 1” hexagonal center cutout and six slots around the outer face for fin connection. Secondly, a hex bar shaft was inserted into the center hexagonal cutout of one of the wheels. Then, aluminum fins were inserted into two adjacent slots of the acrylic wheel, flush against the internal shaft. Lastly, the second plastic wheel was placed onto the opposite side of the first plastic wheel, inserting the shaft  and aluminum fins into the slots on this second wheel. 

Then, our primary container was altered using a dremel and hand saw to cut out semicircle shapes on opposite sides of the bucket to mount the ‘water wheel’s’ internal rod and cut out a rectangular opening near a bottom corner of the bucket to allow water and grapes to pass through. Finally, the entire assembly was completed by mounting the ‘water wheel’ into the cut out semicircles.
